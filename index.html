How it works

Everyone has an A4 sheet somewhere, and A-series paper is defined by ISO 216, so its dimensions are the same everywhere. Fold a sheet in half and you get the next size down (A4 → A5 → A6 …). Join sheets together and you can build the bigger sizes (2A0 and 4A0). Every size has a known long edge, so any length can be written as a sum of those edges.

Finding the fewest sheets. This is a cousin of the Frobenius coin problem: the sheet lengths are the “coins” and the length you want is the “amount”. Two methods are included:

  • Dynamic programming. For every length from 1 up to your target, it works out the smallest number of sheets that makes exactly that length, building on the answers for shorter lengths. Then it walks back from the target to see which sheets were used. This always finds a minimum, and is fast for any length you’d realistically measure.
  • Branch & bound. Tries the biggest sheets first, then backs off one sheet at a time and moves on to the next size down, throwing away any attempt that already uses as many sheets as the best answer so far. Also exact, but slower on very large lengths (this page stops it after a few million attempts).

The original Python had three solvers, but the “full” and “memory-efficient” versions do the same sum and always give the same answer, so they’re combined here as one.

Reading the answer. A(-2×4, -1×1, 6×1) means four sheets of size −2 (4A0), one of size −1 (2A0) and one of size 6 (A6). Sizes are shown in the same numbering as the code, where 0 is A0, −1 is 2A0 and −2 is 4A0.

Going backwards. The “Sheets to length” tool simply multiplies each size’s long edge by the number of sheets and adds them up. You can paste an answer from the calculator straight into it to check the sum.

Impossible lengths. The smallest sheet in the standard is A10 at 37 mm, so nothing shorter can be made, and a patchwork of gaps remains above that. Since the sheet lengths share no common factor above 1, there is a point past which every length works: with sizes 4A0 to A10 it is above 695 mm. Including the folded sizes A11 (26 mm), A12 (18 mm) and A13 (13 mm) closes the gaps for everything above 100 mm. Use the tool above to see the exact gaps.

Lengths are rounded to the nearest whole millimetre, as the paper sizes are themselves rounded to whole millimetres. Real-world accuracy depends on your folding.
